\section{Limitations and Future Research}

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the
empirical evaluation involved only twelve participants across three groups and focused on a single
hidden-profile scenario. Although this setting provided a controlled environment for examining the system's
ability to support information exchange and decision-making, the findings cannot be generalized to other
meeting contexts or group constellations. Moreover, the short duration of the sessions and the reliance on
immediate post-meeting questionnaires limited the assessment of long-term acceptance and the system's effects
on collaboration and decision-making.

Second, the high rate of interaction limits what can be concluded from the evaluation. Across the three
sessions the prototype produced between eleven and nineteen spoken contributions, or about 0.5 to 1.0
interventions per minute (\autoref{tab:intervention-frequency}). Participants predominantly perceived these
contributions as disruptive and, after repeated interruptions, began to disregard the agent. The meeting
among the participants was still rated favorably, but the system itself was perceived unfavorably. Under
that condition, a useful contribution is difficult to distinguish from one that went unheard because trust
had already declined. Positive effects of proactive moderation can therefore remain invisible. The findings
describe this prototype at the observed frequency. They leave open whether a sparser and more selective
pattern of intervention would be accepted, and whether it would support information sharing and decision
quality.

Third, the prototype was subject to technical limitations that affected the interaction between the AI agent
and participants. Its reliance on text-based transcripts restricted its ability to recognize contextual cues
beyond the spoken content, while transcription errors and response latency sometimes reduced the relevance
and timing of interventions. Furthermore, the system could not reliably manage situations in which
participants spoke simultaneously or continued speaking while the agent intervened. The intervention prompts,
thresholds, and turn-allocation mechanisms were also based on heuristic decisions and have not yet been
systematically validated in the context of goal-oriented meetings.

Fourth, research on spoken AI interaction was not treated as a distinct part of the literature review. The
account of proactive behavior drew primarily on chatbot-oriented work, in particular the Inner Thoughts
framework, which was designed and evaluated for asynchronous text conversation
(\autoref{sec:proactive-ai-interactions}; \cite{liuProactiveConversationalAgents2025}). Spoken multi-party
meetings impose further demands. A response is expected to fit the current moment, turns overlap, and an
ongoing contribution of the agent can be interrupted. These conditions were not examined in their own right,
so assumptions about when and how an agent should take a turn were carried over from chat to voice. The user
studies indicate that this transfer holds only in part. Facilitation decisions and the mechanics of spoken
interaction proved difficult to separate, which limits how far the observed difficulties can be attributed
to the facilitation logic alone.

Future research should address these limitations by evaluating the system with larger and more diverse
samples across different meeting scenarios and formats. This would help determine whether the observed
patterns also apply to other convergent tasks, such as prioritization or problem-solving. Such studies
should also vary the intervention rate. A comparison between the frequency observed here and a deliberately
sparse pattern would show whether participants take up facilitative contributions when the agent speaks less
often, and whether acceptance and decision quality change with that frequency. Further technical development
should focus on improving contextual awareness, reducing latency, and ensuring that interventions are both
relevant and appropriately timed. In addition, systematic testing of prompts, thresholds, and turn-allocation
strategies could help establish more reliable intervention mechanisms. The potential use of additional
contextual signals may also be explored, while carefully considering privacy and participants' concerns about
surveillance.

Work on comparable systems should also draw on research into spoken multi-party interaction, including
overlap, interruption, and real-time turn-taking. An architecture that already provides these functions
would offer a firmer basis on which facilitation logic can be tested. Beyond technical improvements, future
studies should investigate how the level of proactivity influences user acceptance and meeting outcomes.
Finally, longer-term studies in realistic meeting environments with a more surficant an dspecialeded system would
provide valuable insights into sustained acceptance and the effects of proactive AI moderation on
participation, information sharing, communication quality, and decision-making.

Overall, the prototype demonstrates the potential of proactive AI moderation to support goal-oriented remote
meetings, while highlighting the challenges of intervening in a context-sensitive and unobtrusive manner.
Further research is necessary to determine how AI systems can provide selective and well-timed facilitation
without disrupting the natural flow of discussion or limiting participants' autonomy.
