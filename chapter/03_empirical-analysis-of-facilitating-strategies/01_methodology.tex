\section{Methodology: Expert Interviews}
\label{sec:interview-methodology}

As described in the theoretical foundations, interaction in meetings encompasses several levels
at once: psychological effects, personality differences, and communication among the
participants.

It remains to be seen how this will be handled in practice, which factors will be the focus, how facilitators
will conduct themselves in meetings involving convergent decision-making, and which practices can be
translated into the actual behavior of an AI-powered meeting system.

The interviews were intended to facilitate precisely this transition. Their purpose was, on the one hand, to
move from the general descriptions of meetings and facilitation in the literature to the concrete experiences
of practicing facilitators, in order to identify a smaller set of characteristics relevant to an AI agent, as
well as to uncover additional insights, which may not yet have been captured by the literature review,
regarding what an AI agent should pay attention to and where its limitations might be.

Semi-structured expert interviews were chosen as the method. This type of interview allows for in-depth
insights into the respondents’ perspectives through open-ended questions and flexible follow-ups, while at the same time
ensuring a thematic focus through a guide.

Expert interviews are particularly suitable because experts, through their specialized knowledge and
practical experience, provide efficient access to specific areas of knowledge \cite[p.
877]{baurHandbuchMethodenEmpirischen2022}. The definition of an expert is context-dependent and must be
determined on a case-by-case basis depending on the research topic \cite[p.
877]{baurHandbuchMethodenEmpirischen2022}. In this study, only individuals with verifiable professional
experience in the field of facilitation and who are professionally active in this context were interviewed.
Therefore, the interviews conducted can be classified as semi-structured expert interviews.

\subsection{Participants: Selection of 3 facilitating experts}

Three experts were interviewed for this study. Their recruitment involved a longer process that relied on
personal outreach. At the beginning of the study, local events and online meetups in the facilitation
industry were attended. This made it possible, on the one hand, to establish direct contacts with practicing
facilitators and, on the other hand, to gain in-depth insight into the facilitation industry as well as into
consulting services in the context of organizational development.

Key events included a two-day conference on cooperation and New Work in Darmstadt (June), attended by
facilitators and organizational designers from all over Germany, as well as regular participation in a
monthly, two-hour online meetup for facilitators (since April), where the future prospects of facilitation
and, in particular, the potential of AI in the field of facilitation are discussed.

The deliberate establishment of personal contacts served two purposes: First, it allowed for an initial
assessment of which experts could provide relevant input on the research question. Second, the
pre-existing personal acquaintance helped foster an open and trusting atmosphere for the discussions. No
details about this study were shared with the selected experts prior to the interviews in order to avoid biasing them.

The following criteria were considered when selecting the experts:
\begin{itemize}{}
  \item Active professional practice in the field of facilitation with at least five years of professional experience
  \item Regular work in remote-based formats
  \item Regular facilitation of groups in decision-making meetings
  \item Occasional use of AI to support their work as facilitators
\end{itemize}

Beyond these common criteria, the three experts were specifically selected so that their areas of focus
differ slightly and each covers an aspect relevant to the research question.

Expert~1 is an external facilitator and the managing director of a consulting firm. Her focus is on
facilitation and AI-supported facilitation, as well as the future possibilities that arise from them. She has
already conducted several experiments with larger groups in which voice agents were used as a supportive element.

Expert~2 works in organizational development. He facilitates internal meetings within his own organization as
well as client workshops in which groups reach decisions.

Expert~3 serves as a facilitator for large-scale projects and executive teams, both online and offline, often
in change initiatives, such as those involving communication, stakeholder engagement, and collaboration within
project teams. Additionally, one of his areas of focus is mediation, that is conflict resolution and
consensus-building within groups. For decision-making processes, how and when to intervene in the group is
particularly relevant.

\subsection{Data Collection: Conduction of semi-structured interviews (30 min)}

Both the order of the various sections and the questions within the sections follow a structure that
progresses from the general to the specific. The questions are open-ended in order to avoid dictating too
much or merely receiving confirmation of what is already known from the literature review. According to
Helfferich in Baur et al., providing too much guidance is one of the biggest mistakes in conducting
qualitative interviews. \cite[p. 878]{baurHandbuchMethodenEmpirischen2022}

On the other hand, Helfferich also notes that it is in the interest of research to limit openness. \cite[p.
878f.]{baurHandbuchMethodenEmpirischen2022} For this reason, the scope of the study (see
\autoref{fig:scope-thesis}) was explained to the interviewees both at the time of the initial request and
prior to the interviews.

The interview guide (see \autoref{annex:interview-guide-facilitators}) is divided into five sections. In the
first section, the interviewees were asked to describe their professional context. This served to further
validate their expertise and also to provide a simple way to begin the conversation.
This was followed by a section focusing on challenges in targeted remote meetings involving decision-making,
which led into a section where participants were asked about specific facilitation strategies. The final
section focused on requirements for an AI-supported meeting facilitation system.
A conscious decision was also made to include questions about how facilitators would envision such an
AI-supported system. One could argue that facilitators are not experts in this area and that this could lead
to speculative, uninformed answers, since they are not aware of the technical capabilities and limitations of
AI-supported systems.
However, the questions are phrased in such a way that facilitators can describe the behaviors and
characteristics of this system, which is within their area of expertise, without needing detailed knowledge
of the technical implementation. Additionally, in Houtti et al., a co-host design was developed with
participants in a meeting to determine how meeting participants would like such a system to function. Here,
the facilitator’s perspective is included.

Before the actual interview, a practice interview was conducted with another student to assess the general
clarity of the questions and the approximate time frame. However, since the subject matter was specialized,
another run-through was simulated using ChatGPT in voice mode as a practice partner.

The actual interviews were conducted via Zoom.

The interviews were always scheduled to last exactly one hour. The interview guide was initially designed to
last 40 minutes, to allow for some leeway.

The interviews were scheduled to last approximately 40–50 minutes in order to allow for a time buffer in case
of unexpectedly detailed responses. The very first interview confirmed the need for this buffer, as some of
the participants’ responses were longer than expected. The first interview significantly exceeded the planned
duration. The actual interview durations ranged from 50 minutes (Interview 3) to 75 minutes (Interview 1),
with Interview 2 lasting 65 minutes.

No more than one interview was conducted per day. After an interview was completed, notes were taken
immediately to capture initial thoughts while the impressions from the interview were still fresh.

\subsection{Analysis: Thematic coding}
\label{sec:interview-coding}

The interviews were analyzed by inductive thematic coding.
The codebook was created after the interviews had been transcribed.
The three transcripts were read and codes were formulated from those claims.
While the transcripts were worked through, the codebook was extended when a passage required a new
distinction, and definitions were tightened where neighboring codes overlapped.
Passages already coded were then checked against the revised scheme.

The final Codebook with Rules, definitions, and shortened anchor quotations in the original German wording
are documented in \autoref{annex:expert-interview-codebook}. A passage can get coded with multiple codes.

Fifteen codes are grouped into four categories. Decision conditions (D) describe what has to be true around a
decision, including work before and after the meeting: decision scope, process and rights (D1), preparation and
follow-up (D2), and
structural limits the meeting itself cannot remove (D3).
Meeting dynamics (M) describe what puts goal orientation or decision quality at risk during the
session: focus, time, and a decision that is not yet ripe (M1), voices, hierarchy, and dissent
(M2), and remote conditions, including ambiguous signals (M3).
A claim can carry both a D code and an M code: D is what the meeting depends on or cannot remove,
and M is what that does while people are together, so the clock is not the cut.
Facilitation practice (F) describes what the experts do in response and is the source of the
requirements: process structure (F1), a visible state of the discussion (F2), bringing in a missing
voice or fact (F3), the judgment of when and whom to address (F4), and neutrality, so that the
decision stays with the group or a named owner (F5).
AI co-facilitator (A) records the system: useful functions (A1), role, trigger, and acceptance
(A2), risks and non-goals (A3), and context that lies outside the transcript (A4).

The unit of coding is one coherent claim.
It may run across consecutive turns of the same speaker and may receive more than one code, for
instance when it states both a problem and a response.
Interviewer turns, consent, scheduling, and biographical self-description were left uncoded.
One coder carried out the coding.
