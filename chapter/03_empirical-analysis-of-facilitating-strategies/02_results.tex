This section reports the thematic analysis of the three transcribed expert interviews.
E1 (interview~1) works in organizational development, strategy, and multi-stakeholder
processes, and combines facilitation with consulting~\eref{e1#15, e1#16, e1#17}.
E2 (interview~2) maintains internal decision routines and facilitates commissioned
workshops~\eref{e2#6, e2#8}.
E3 (interview~3) facilitates large change projects and leadership teams, online and in
person~\eref{e3#8, e3#10}.
Names of persons and organizations that appear in the transcripts are not repeated here.
The interviews were coded with the codebook in \autoref{annex:expert-interview-codebook},
developed inductively from the transcripts as described in \autoref{sec:interview-coding}.
Codes were applied to claims by the interviewee.
One claim could receive several codes.
Interviewer turns, consent, and biographical self-description were left uncoded.
One coder did the coding.
No second coder was used, and nothing in this section is offered as a frequency beyond
these three accounts.
Consecutive rows often continue a single thought.
Those rows are read as one line of argument.
A marker such as \eref{e2#4} locates row~4 in the structured transcript of interview~2.
Where a conclusion rests on more than one passage, the marker lists those rows.
The markers are locators in the interview material, not literature citations.

The interviews were asked to serve two purposes.
They describe how a remote group stays with a joint decision, and they are a source of
requirements for an AI moderator.
Both purposes are reported here.
Findings that later fall outside the prototype are included, because they bound what an
in-meeting system can be asked to do.
Design consequences are not settled in this section.

\subsection{What has to be true around the decision}
\label{sec:interview-decision-conditions}

This subsection reports conditions the live discussion depends on.
Each condition is stated once.
Where the same condition disturbs the hour, \autoref{sec:interview-meeting-dynamics}
reports only that disturbance.

The first condition is a shared information base, and a goal that has been said (D2).
E1 calls lack of preparation the largest failure of remote meetings: people enter without
having read the document, the group has no basis for a decision, and the meeting is
postponed~\eref{e1#24, e1#26, e1#27}.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{That happened to me again just this week. We waited four weeks for a meeting, [\ldots] so that
      five people could coordinate, and then three of them walk in and say, \enquote{Yeah, oh, I'm afraid I
    didn't read the document.}}~\eref{e1#25}
\end{quote}}
She treats the meeting as an investment of organizational time, and she treats unprepared
attendance as a waste of that investment~\eref{e1#26, e1#28}.
For her, what happens before the meeting is the central part of the work, and she does it
with the people who carry responsibility for the meeting~\eref{e1#122}.
E2 makes the same observation.
People will not enter a workshop unprepared, and they regularly enter a meeting
unprepared~\eref{e2#13}.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{[\ldots] people usually go into a meeting unprepared.}~\eref{e2#13}
\end{quote}}
The invitation often contains no information, so participants cannot tell what is to be
decided, what their role is, or why they were invited~\eref{e2#28, e2#30}.
E2 estimates that at least half of the work is preparation: the concrete problem, who must be
present, whether the decision owner is in the room, and which decision procedure will be
used~\eref{e2#90}.
E3 puts the same weight on the briefing with the decision-maker~\eref{e3#47, e3#84}.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{The most important thing isn't what you do during the meeting itself. The most important thing
    is what you clarify with your manager beforehand. What is the real goal?}~\eref{e3#84}
\end{quote}}
Goals and non-goals have to be stated in that preparation~\eref{e3#47, e3#86, e3#88}.
Leaders, in E3's account, often have the goal in mind and still fail to say it~\eref{e3#86, e3#88}.
For a short slot with senior decision makers, E3 prepares a decision memorandum: the dilemma,
options including the option of doing nothing, and the costs and benefits of
each~\eref{e3#47, e3#48, e3#49}.

The second condition is that the decision is specified before the discussion starts (D1).
The specification has three parts.
The group has to know what is being decided, and of what kind.
It has to know who has the right to decide, including who decides if the group does not agree,
and that person has to be in the meeting.
It has to know the procedure, and the scope that procedure actually leaves to the people in
the room.

(1) What is being decided takes more than one form.
E1 distinguishes documented formal decisions from informal agreements that rest on trust, and
she says that many of the decisions in her workshops are decisions about next steps rather
than a final choice~\eref{e1#20, e1#21, e1#73, e1#86}.
The point to be decided, and the level at which it sits, has to be named in advance.
A choice that looks technical still carries choices about processes, roles, and
tasks~\eref{e1#128, e1#129}.
E2 separates reversible decisions, which can be tried under a ``safe enough to try'' rule,
from irreversible ones, which justify a longer and more careful process~\eref{e2#40, e2#80, e2#94}.
Not deciding is, for E2, itself a decision, because delay has a cost~\eref{e2#80, e2#168}.

(2) Who may decide is stated most directly by E3.
E3 asks who has the authority, including budget authority, and who is responsible if the
meeting ends without a decision~\eref{e3#51, e3#53}.
If the team does not agree, E3 puts that responsibility on the leader, who then has to decide
alone~\eref{e3#53}.
For a team that is meant to decide together, the same briefing asks how far the team is
actually allowed to go~\eref{e3#49, e3#50}.
E2 makes presence part of the same condition.
The first question for a decision meeting is whether the person who can decide is in the room.
If not, the group needs an explicit way to handle that absence, or it will leave without a
decision~\eref{e2#75, e2#90}.
People who will be affected, and who are not in the room, are a further gap.
E2's example is a shift decided by the people in the meeting and handed to the person who has
to work it.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{They are not sitting in the meeting at all. [\ldots] I come back from the meeting,
      and, Oliver, your new shift is 1~p.m.\ to 5~p.m. You now work from 1~p.m.\ to 9~p.m.
      You were not heard. [\ldots] A week later your resignation is on the
    table.}~\eref{e2#122}
\end{quote}}

Auf der anderen seite können auch zu viele irellevante Perosnen eingeladen sein.

When the exchange does not happen, the people who only listen will notice it.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{One person speaks and the others listen, and they ask themselves, well, I have not
    even said anything. Why was I actually invited?}~\eref{e2#103}
\end{quote}}

(3) The procedure is the point E2 develops furthest.
In E2's account, organizations usually know two procedures, a decision by authority and a
decision by majority~\eref{e2#52, e2#53}.
Majority is described as a poor fit for most organizational choices, because it produces
winners and losers and can exclude a minority whose cooperation the result
needs~\eref{e2#54, e2#119}.
E2 prefers consensus for fundamental questions, systemic consensus when several options are
on the table, and a consultative individual decision when one or two people hold the
expertise: they must hear the others, and they then decide and carry the
responsibility~\eref{e2#119, e2#120, e2#121, e2#148}.
``Disagree and commit'' is named as a practice that keeps dissent from turning into a refusal
to implement~\eref{e2#40, e2#41, e2#117}.
The procedure is empty when the scope behind it is only apparent.
E2's example is a meeting that formalizes a decision already taken~\eref{e2#26, e2#28}.
E3 describes leaders who tell a team that the decision is theirs when it has already been
made, and who want the team only to nod it through~\eref{e3#50}.
E3's advice is to say so in advance~\eref{e3#50}.
E1 describes hierarchies that have decided and do not want to hear where the decision does
not fit~\eref{e1#45, e1#46}.
In all three accounts, a meeting that asks for commitment without a real scope of decision
damages the result~\eref{e1#45, e2#26, e3#50}.

The third condition is a limit the meeting cannot repair (D3).
Organizational structure shapes the hour from outside it.
A structural problem can disturb the decision, and it can be the reason a meeting exists.
Such a structure is hard to remove inside the meeting.
E2 states it most sharply.
Many meetings exist because decision rights are missing: small choices wait for the next
regular meeting, people who could decide are not allowed to, and the meeting becomes the
symptom of that structure~\eref{e2#9, e2#10, e2#65, e2#73}.
Better moderation, on this account, can support a meeting and still leave the structural
problem in place~\eref{e2#74}.
The absence of the person who can decide, already stated as part of who may decide, is for E2
also the sign of that structure~\eref{e2#75}.

Manchmal ist das Meeting ansich bereits nicht das korrekte foramt.
E2 also treats meetings that only broadcast information as a misuse of synchronous
time~\eref{e2#102, e2#103}.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{Far too many meetings are misused simply to pass on information.}~\eref{e2#103}
\end{quote}}
E1's version of the same limit is the guest list.
Standard meetings contain people who cannot contribute and who cannot
decide~\eref{e1#29, e1#30, e1#32}.
Hearing something is not, for her, a reason to attend, because a short protocol can do that
afterwards~\eref{e1#31}.
She also says that an external facilitator can invite dissent for the duration of a workshop
but cannot change a fear-based culture~\eref{e1#56, e1#57}.
These limits are felt while the group is together.
The speaking pattern they produce is reported in \autoref{sec:interview-meeting-dynamics}.

Laut E2 ist der synchrnoe austausch der
Hauptgrnd für meetings. das wird aber nciht benötigt um infach nur informationen weiter zugeben.

The same outside work continues after the meeting closes (D2).
A consequential decision has a before and an after: options have to be clarified, and someone
has to implement the result by a stated time~\eref{e3#26}.
The decision is written into the system the organization already uses, so that people who
were not in the room can act on it~\eref{e3#27, e3#28}.
Ownership belongs with that handoff.
A result that the leadership team does not take as its own is, in E1's words,
waste~\eref{e1#137}.
Ownership comes from having worked on the result and from hearing one's own voice in it.
It does not come from receiving a finished proposal from above, or from leaving the content
with a consultant~\eref{e1#135, e1#136, e1#139}.
E2 states the operational form: who does what by when, and where the group checks that it
was done~\eref{e2#133, e2#134, e2#135}.
A decision that nobody follows is, for E2, the worst outcome~\eref{e2#133}.
E3 separates that ownership from the facilitator's role.
That boundary is taken up in \autoref{sec:interview-facilitation-practice}~\eref{e3#89, e3#90}.

\subsection{What puts the decision at risk while the group is together}
\label{sec:interview-meeting-dynamics}

Given the conditions in \autoref{sec:interview-decision-conditions}, three things still happen
while the group is together: loss of the question, suppression of relevant dissent, and
signals that are hard to read at a distance, somethimes caues by the remote setting~\eref{e1#37, e2#114, e3#30}.
Some are the in-room face of a condition already stated.
This subsection reports that disturbance.
The condition it comes from stays in the preceding subsection.

Loss of the question, and the time in which the group can stay with it (M1), takes several forms.
A side track because the decision is not yet ripe is the in-room face of a missing question or
of missing preparation.
The claim here is the drift, and the close that comes too early.
E3 names the side track directly.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{What can of course happen is that side tracks keep getting opened. Perhaps because
    the decision simply is not ripe yet, even if it was prepared.}~\eref{e3#30}
\end{quote}}
In a group of ten or twelve with no structure, the same loss shows up as attention.
Half of them start writing emails or answering messages and are not listening at all.
E3 calls that a main problem, and calls managing the participants' attention
essential~\eref{e3#31}.
E2 locates the same loss in the day around the meeting.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{The meeting is embedded in twenty other meetings in the day, so there is no pause
    from one topic to the next. My head is not free.}~\eref{e2#31}
\end{quote}}
The previous meeting overruns, and the switch from one context to the next cannot be
processed~\eref{e2#32}.
E1 describes the same side-work, writing emails while half listening, as the absence of a
contribution~\eref{e1#41}.
For her the loss matters because attention is what carries the process.
When people are only functionally present, nothing happens~\eref{e1#95, e1#98}.
E2 describes the drift in the same terms: the group is no longer talking about the actual
problem and the decision.
In the course of that divergence the group can notice that it has been working on the wrong
question, and that it no longer makes sense to hold the original goal~\eref{e2#131, e2#132}.
What follows is a judgment: go back to the start, or, for the moment, work with the question
the group has now arrived at~\eref{e2#132}.
The interviews asked about the convergent phase.
The experts keep tying that phase to the open phase and to other meeting types.
M1 is therefore a passage between phases, as well as a loss of the decision itself.
E1 describes the moment when new information has just come back from small groups.
That return is, for her, usually the most critical point, because the group is again in the
open phase of Kaner's diamond and has to find its way toward a decision~\eref{e1#72, e1#73}.
E2 names what sits between opening and closing.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{In between is the groan zone, this groan zone that is so painful. [\ldots] Now we
      argue, we discuss, and so on, and as a facilitator I have to endure that. I must not close
      it or force it. And of course there is time pressure. [\ldots] If I only have 45~minutes,
      but a complex topic, I spend too little time in that zone, and yet we now have to come to
    a decision quickly.}~\eref{e2#113, e2#114}
\end{quote}}
So it is important to find the right balance to get every body heard in the given time frame.
E1 makes the same observation: once the content starts, the hour is already
gone, and the alternative is fewer people and more time~\eref{e1#105, e1#106}.
E3 adds a timing rule at the other end.
Reopening a large controversy in the last third of a workshop is dangerous.
The move then is to stop discussing the substance and to discuss only how a decision will
be reached~\eref{e3#45}.

Dissent and hierarchy (M2) are described as conditions of decision quality, not as a problem
of politeness.
The fear culture that one session cannot change, and the hierarchy that has already decided,
were stated in \autoref{sec:interview-decision-conditions}.
Both act inside the hour, on who speaks and on what follows when a needed voice does not.
E1 says that few organizations treat contradiction as useful, because a contradiction shows
where something does not fit~\eref{e1#44}.
An objection spoken in the room is, for her, what the meeting is there to hear, and she names
invited criticism as a success factor of a good meeting~\eref{e1#43, e1#156}.
What is not invited does not disappear.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{Then it stays at the bottom, it bubbles away down there, and at some point it
      splashes up and turns into a conflict very quickly, which is
    unnecessary.}~\eref{e1#46}
\end{quote}}
The same failure has a form she treats as a mark of quality.
If the meeting simply starts, and only one or two people speak, the voices that are always
heard take the floor~\eref{e1#68}.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{Then the ones speak up who are extroverted anyway, and for whom it comes easily.
      Those are the voices one always hears. And the others stay nicely silent in the background.
    You know that this is often exactly how it goes.}~\eref{e1#69}
\end{quote}}
Trust is, for her, the condition of being able to say that something is wrong, and it is
harder when the people have never met, as in a remote setting~\eref{e1#43}.
She also describes the dissent that does not help: chronic objection and the habit of taking a substantive
criticism as a personal attack~\eref{e1#51, e1#53}.
For E2's critical moments are participation failures.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{People do not take part. They have no way to take part: they do not get to speak,
      or the method does not bring them in. Or they do not want to take part. So participation
    is a problem.}~\eref{e2#51}
\end{quote}}

He ties that to the reason for being in the room together.
The point of meeting at the same time is the exchange, and hearing everyone.
A message can carry information. It cannot replace that exchange~\eref{e2#102}.

The procedure can produce the same failure.
A majority, already described as a procedure that produces losers, leaves people who then
have to live with a decision they lost~\eref{e2#54}.
Where a dissent is not carried, E2 describes the meeting as a fight that makes winners and
losers.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{Otherwise decisions become winner-and-loser fights, culturally, and then you say
      in your team: yes, I saw it differently in the meeting. That was not my opinion, but the
    others outvoted me.}~\eref{e2#41}
\end{quote}}

E3 defines the relevant voices that should get heard by what would happen afterwards.
Where people notice, during the meeting, that they are only there to nod a decision through,
he says the team registers it quickly and the result is discontent~\eref{e3#50}.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{The people who should get to speak are those who, if they have a criticism of the
      decision, could credibly cast doubt on it. [\ldots] If a decision is taken that does not
      suit them, they would afterwards say, I'll do it differently anyway, even if they do not
    do so openly.}~\eref{e3#68}
\end{quote}}
The hierarchy shapes the order in which agreement appears.
In some settings a lower rank will not agree until the senior person has agreed, while in
others the senior person stays noncommittal until everyone else has spoken~\eref{e3#77, e3#79}.

The remote setting changes which of these signs can be seen (M3).

E1 prefers to do complex content in person~\eref{e1#23}.
What she loses online is the reading she has in the room.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{Especially when I am sitting there with 16 tiles, or more, and facilitating them,
      a certain feel for this group and for the people is lost to me as a facilitator. [\ldots]
      I can perceive mood, resistance, agreement, and so on much better when I am in a room with
    them.}~\eref{e1#37}
\end{quote}}
Camera-off participation and parallel work are, for her, forms of
absence~\eref{e1#40, e1#41, e1#99}.
A black tile is something she does not accept, because the group then cannot tell whether
a person is present~\eref{e1#40, e1#41}.
Language adds a further gap.
A meeting in a shared foreign language, with more than one cultural habit of what may be
said, makes a common picture harder to reach~\eref{e1#34, e1#35, e1#36}.
E3 describes a repair that a face-to-face break still allows and an online break drops.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{If I take a break online, people are disconnected at first, and then the smaller
      points may not get clarified. If people disagree, or, \enquote{hey, what did you mean
    earlier?} Those things do not come up.}~\eref{e3#19}
\end{quote}}
The agreement itself becomes harder to read.
Especially online, E3 finds it very difficult to judge whether people are really in
favor~\eref{e3#69}.
The sign is a thinner ``yes'': people grow quieter, or less enthusiastic, as the decision
approaches, and a ``yes'' that already names the next action is a different commitment from
a flat one~\eref{e3#70, e3#72, e3#76}.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{A \enquote{yeah} and a \enquote{yes} are two very different things.}~\eref{e3#74}
\end{quote}}
E3 refuses a single reading of that sign.
The same word means different things for different people~\eref{e3#76, e3#79}.
Shared visual tools are, for E3, a condition of a complex decision online, and so is the
time required for people who do not yet know the tool~\eref{e3#22, e3#24, e3#25}.
Technical failure across organizations, including the ordinary failure of a microphone, is
named as a further remote obstacle~\eref{e3#20}.
E2 is more sparing on the remote medium.
The point that does appear is parallel work while half listening, which a workshop makes
harder than a meeting~\eref{e2#21}.
Some methods, in the same account, also depend on a shared board.
Without one, E2 cannot run them~\eref{e2#36}.
The remote-specific material is therefore strongest in E1 and E3.
The material is a list of cues and repairs that become unreliable when the group is not in
one room~\eref{e1#37, e3#19, e3#74}.

\subsection{What the facilitators do in the convergent phase}
\label{sec:interview-facilitation-practice}

The practices are reported here as practices.
They are the material from which requirements can be taken.
They are not yet requirements.

Although the focus was on the convergent phase of the meeting, the experts repeatedly drew connections to
other phases of the meeting. This suggests that these phases are more closely interlinked than previously
assumed. When connections were made to areas that did not strictly pertain exclusively to the convergent
phase, these were nevertheless mentioned here to illustrate the interplay.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Facilitation moves in the convergent phase.}
  \label{tab:interview-facilitation-moves}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}p{5.6cm}>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}X@{}}
    \toprule
    Facilitation action & What the facilitator attends to \\
    \midrule
    Open before closing (F1) & Whether the point is understood before evaluation \\
    Make the state visible (F2) & Whether everyone can see where the group stands \\
    Bring in a voice or a fact (F3) & Which missing voice or fact would change the result \\
    Judge when to move (F4) & Whether this moment can carry a decision \\
    Leave the decision with the group (F5) & Whether the result becomes the group's own \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The shared shape of a convergent meeting (F1) is to open before closing, and to keep
clarification separate from debate~\eref{e1#72, e2#33, e3#45}.
E2 states the sequence as information, then a check that people mean the same thing, then
perspectives, and only then evaluation~\eref{e2#33, e2#44, e2#46}.
Jumping from a document into debate is named as a source of decisions that people would
have made differently if they had understood the point~\eref{e2#33, e2#34}.
E1 uses a check-in so that every person has spoken once before the extroverted voices take
the floor, and small groups so that people can think by speaking~\eref{e1#65, e1#66, e1#69, e1#70}.
She describes the overall shape as a diamond: build the complexity, then bring it back to a
decision or to next steps~\eref{e1#72, e1#73}.
E3 follows a prepared plan and leaves it when the discussion becomes controversial in a way
the plan did not expect~\eref{e3#61}.
Early or mid-way, the two sides may go into separate groups to sharpen what they do not
understand in the other position~\eref{e3#44}.
Near the end, the structure changes level.
The group stops reworking the controversy and agrees the path to a decision: which queries,
who fetches which information by when, and when they meet again~\eref{e3#45}.

Making the state visible (F2) is the convergent move all three describe in concrete terms.
E1 clusters results on a shared board and then summarizes: given what has been heard, can
the group agree these next steps~\eref{e1#82, e1#85}.
The summary is offered as a reading of the group's material~\eref{e1#84, e1#85}.
E2 has people write pros and cons, or the consequences of a candidate decision, so that the
option space is outside any one person's memory~\eref{e2#36, e2#38}.
With more than a few people, writing or a small group is also a way to keep the contributions
in view~\eref{e2#86, e2#88}.
E3's criterion is that everyone should see, at one glance, where everyone
stands~\eref{e3#56, e3#57}.
A serial round in which each person says an opinion is, in a larger group, a practice E3
rejects, because people stop listening~\eref{e3#56, e3#57}.
Scales, a fist-to-five that includes an explicit veto, a matrix of effort against value, and
a poll are ways to put that picture in front of the group~\eref{e3#40, e3#42, e3#55, e3#57}.
E3 also paraphrases near the close, sometimes slightly past what was said, in order to test
whether something is still unsaid~\eref{e3#81}.
The summary and the display are checks.
They are not the decision~\eref{e1#85, e3#42, e3#90}.

Bringing a voice or a fact in (F3) is selective.
E1 invites people who have not yet spoken, and she prepares questions that are meant to
produce a new angle on the topic~\eref{e1#71, e1#88}.
She also asks leadership, or a product owner, to say what they will do with what they have
heard~\eref{e1#76, e1#83}.
The invitation of a quiet person is, for her, easier in the room than online, where it can
feel intrusive, and it can stigmatize, especially across culture and age~\eref{e1#88, e1#89, e1#90}.
Naming introversion as the reason is something she would do cautiously~\eref{e1#89, e1#90}.
E2's corresponding rules are to hear lower rank before senior rank, so that the senior view
does not set the room, and to let everyone speak before anyone
evaluates~\eref{e2#46, e2#111, e2#112}.
A role, such as a skeptic's, is a way to make a critical view sayable~\eref{e2#45}.
If a needed role is absent, E2 will ask the group what that role would be likely to say, and
will mark the answer as a hypothesis~\eref{e2#77, e2#78}.
E3 will not invite every silent person.
An intern who says nothing is left.
A co-founder who has been silent on a topic that matters to him is asked, by name, after a
short restatement of what has been said~\eref{e3#35, e3#36}.
The people who are asked are those whose later resistance would matter~\eref{e3#68}.
Alongside speech, E1 and E2 both use writing and chat, because some people think before they
can speak and others think by speaking~\eref{e1#70, e1#102, e2#86, e2#87}.
E3's minimum, when a speech is not needed, is still a visible degree of confidence~\eref{e3#69}.

The judgment of when to move (F4) is described as restraint as much as intervention.
E2 has to endure the groan zone and not force a close~\eref{e2#114, e2#115}.
E3 waits, counts about seven seconds, and only then asks in another way or repeats the
paraphrase~\eref{e3#81, e3#82}.
A non-answer is sometimes the information.
E3 stops pushing when further questioning would be delicate~\eref{e3#119}.
E1 watches whether the group is only functionally present or also engaged, and she treats
absence of energy as a reason the process will stall~\eref{e1#94, e1#95, e1#97}.
She also weighs chronic objection rather than inviting it without limit~\eref{e1#51}.
E2 asks, before an irreversible decision, whether the moment is even a fit one.
The example is late on a Friday, with no decision capacity left~\eref{e2#93, e2#94}.
The same expert uses the state of the people, including hunger, as a reason to distrust the
moment~\eref{e2#98}.
None of the three describes a cue that would justify the same intervention every
time~\eref{e1#90, e2#129, e3#76, e3#79}.

The boundary of the role (F5) is drawn most sharply by E3.
Leaders sometimes want the facilitator to produce the decision and to make people accept it.
E3 refuses that delegation~\eref{e3#89}.
The facilitator's work is to structure the conversation so that there are real options and
so that people can stand behind a result.
Saying yes or no remains the leader's work, because the facilitator is not in that
role~\eref{e3#90}.
E2 draws the boundary at the other end of the system: the decision should not be taken out
of human hands, particularly where the solution is not yet known~\eref{e2#166}.
E1 requires the group to take ownership, and she also describes a practice that crosses the
line E3 draws.
Because her facilitation sits inside consulting, she prepares substantive proposals for how
to continue, and she describes the art of the work as helping to make the decision as well
as advising on it~\eref{e1#107, e1#130, e1#131, e1#135}.
The three accounts agree that the result has to become the group's~\eref{e1#137, e2#166, e3#90}.
They differ on how far the facilitator may put content into that result.
E3 forbids it~\eref{e3#90}.
E1 treats a consulting proposal she has prepared as material on which the group then
decides~\eref{e1#131, e1#135}.

\subsection{Where an AI-System would help, and where it would not}
\label{sec:interview-ai-cofacilitator}

The experts were not asked to design a technical architecture.
They were asked which behavior would be useful, how a system should enter the meeting, and
what it should not do.
\autoref{tab:interview-ai-functions} and \autoref{tab:interview-ai-limits} record which of
them stated which point.
An empty cell means that this expert did not state it.
It does not mean that they rejected it.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Functions the experts described as useful for an AI in or around a convergent
  meeting.}
  \label{tab:interview-ai-functions}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}c>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}Xccc@{}}
    \toprule
    ID & Function & E1 & E2 & E3 \\
    \midrule
    F1 & Fast summary, clustering, or a visual restatement of options & yes &     & yes \\
    F2 & Timekeeping, agenda position, and a prompt to come to the point & yes &     &     \\
    F3 & Walking the group through a decision: question, options, doing nothing,
    pros and cons, or a named procedure &     & yes & yes \\
    F4 & Pointing at a decision the group has not noticed, or at agreement that is
    thinner than it appears &     &     & yes \\
    F5 & A cue that a named bias may be in play &     & yes &     \\
    F6 & A persona that speaks for an absent stakeholder or a missing expertise & yes & yes &     \\
    F7 & Documentation into a knowledge base, a task system, or an approved summary & yes &     & yes \\
    F8 & Help with preparation, or with stating the goal at the start & yes & yes & yes \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

A fast summary, clustering, or a visual restatement of options (F1) is the use for which E1
considers an AI well suited.
Transcription and summary are already available, including the summary a meeting platform
already offers.
What she would add is faster sensemaking of complex material~\eref{e1#151, e1#157}.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{AI can come in in a really exciting way if it manages to make these summaries faster.
      It helps to make sense of complex data more quickly. It can do that perfectly. And that is
    what it is highly suited to.}~\eref{e1#157}
\end{quote}}
The example is a board that would otherwise remain a jumble of notes.
Clustering it by hand is what used to be required.
The board's AI now returns a summary, including across groups, within a
minute~\eref{e1#158, e1#159}.
At the end she would also have the system restate which decisions it has understood, and ask
whether it has understood them correctly.
She calls that a summary function, and she would have the facilitator call it~\eref{e1#175}.
E3's restatement is visual.
Once an option has been taken, the system summarizes it and shows the advantages and
disadvantages~\eref{e3#99, e3#101}.

Timekeeping, agenda position, and a prompt to come to the point (F2) is the other function
E1 singles out.
Interrupting a person who talks too long is a task human moderators dislike, and she would
have the system take it on, as when someone has been speaking for three
minutes~\eref{e1#172, e1#173}.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{Or it says, hey, I've listened to you, now come to the point. What is this actually
    about for you?}~\eref{e1#173}
\end{quote}}
An agenda signal belongs with it.
If the system knew the agenda, it could show whether the group is still in time or already
out of it, and it could say afterwards that the agenda was badly planned~\eref{e1#174}.

Walking the group through a decision (F3) is, for E2, a named procedure once several options
are visible.
Systemic consensus, a resistance score from 0 to 10 on each option, is the procedure he
names~\eref{e2#147, e2#148}.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{I notice there are several options now. I propose we do systemic consensus.}~\eref{e2#147}
\end{quote}}
Enriching the data behind the option space, clarification, and a prompt to ``disagree and
commit'' or to ``safe enough to try'' are further impulses~\eref{e2#164}.
So is the cost of delay: not deciding is itself a decision, and he would have the system say
what the delay costs~\eref{e2#168}.
E3 can imagine a decision mode that the group switches on.
First the decision question, including what happens if nothing is done, then one option at a
time, summarized and shown, including advantages and disadvantages~\eref{e3#99, e3#100, e3#101}.
She finds it hard to imagine putting the value of a skilled facilitator into such a
system~\eref{e3#97}.
The guided decision is a part she can imagine~\eref{e3#100}.

Pointing at a decision the group has not noticed, or at agreement that is thinner than it
appears (F4), is the function E3 calls directing attention.
The system reads the transcript and points at a place where a decision seems to be required,
or where the group is less agreed than it thinks~\eref{e3#101, e3#102}.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{You may need a decision here, because you are not as agreed as you think. Talk about
    that again.}~\eref{e3#102}
\end{quote}}

A cue that a named bias may be in play (F5) is a warning E2 would have the system give while
it listens.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{Look, careful, careful, halo effect, or this or that bias. That would be extremely
    helpful.}~\eref{e2#147}
\end{quote}}

A persona that speaks for an absent stakeholder or a missing expertise (F6) is an invitation
E1 would make.
She would invite an AI persona as a customer into a regular meeting, where that customer
would not be invited, or as a missing expert, such as sales~\eref{e1#164, e1#165, e1#166}.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{Come in with your voice and set out what you have just heard, and what moves
    you.}~\eref{e1#166}
\end{quote}}
She treats the move as hybrid work, in which agents hold roles, and as a different layer from
human facilitation~\eref{e1#167, e1#168, e1#169}.
E2 names the same device for a role that is absent.
An AI persona can play it so that the space is opened again.
His example is a works council that is not in the room, and what it would be likely to think,
anticipated from earlier meetings~\eref{e2#78}.

Documentation into a knowledge base, a task system, or an approved summary (F7) is, for E1,
what should happen to the transcript afterwards.
She would build the transcripts into a knowledge base, through a workflow that files them,
so that the record does not disappear into a single email~\eref{e1#188, e1#190}.
The form she prefers for that filing is a summary a moderator has approved.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{[\ldots] only protocols that a moderator has approved are then filed, not as a
    transcript but as summaries, and anonymized.}~\eref{e1#205}
\end{quote}}
Faster protocols, and building onward from them, are part of the same advantage~\eref{e1#163}.
E3 considers a result protocol certainly useful, and she would integrate it with the task
system the team already uses, such as a kanban board~\eref{e3#97, e3#98}.

Help with preparation, or with stating the goal at the start (F8), is where E1 locates a
main advantage, alongside those protocols~\eref{e1#163}.
E2 says the questions that belong there are the ones people apparently cannot ask themselves:
whether the decision owner is present, and what is needed so that people arrive
prepared.
Those questions are easier to clarify than in a real-time conversation.
A system that listens to the live meeting and then reacts to its complexity he marks as only
a thought~\eref{e2#154, e2#158}.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{Is the decision maker in the room? What do we need so that people come into the
    meeting well prepared?}~\eref{e2#154}
\end{quote}}
E3 can imagine the first ten minutes being used for the system to help establish what the
goal of today's decision is~\eref{e3#117}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Limits the experts placed on an AI that intervenes in a meeting.}
  \label{tab:interview-ai-limits}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}c>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}Xccc@{}}
    \toprule
    ID & Limit & E1 & E2 & E3 \\
    \midrule
    L1 & Sentiment or attendance monitoring becomes surveillance & yes &     &     \\
    L2 & A record chills dissent, or leaves the dissenter marked & yes & yes &     \\
    L3 & Calling on a quiet person can shame, or can misread the silence & yes &     & yes \\
    L4 & People will not yield the floor to a machine, especially a senior person & yes & yes & yes \\
    L5 & The system must not decide, and must not impose a structure nobody asked for &     & yes & yes \\
    L6 & A transcript is not enough context for a sound intervention & yes & yes & yes \\
    L7 & The group has to agree beforehand that interruption is allowed & yes &     &     \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The limits constrain the functions.

Sentiment or attendance monitoring becomes surveillance (L1) for E1.
She would be very cautious about it.
A report that a person has not spoken in the last ten meetings, or a reading of mood and
engagement from the voice, is for her a shift from contact to control, and it raises data
protection~\eref{e1#152, e1#153, e1#155, e1#160}.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{That can very quickly turn into control. [\ldots] the AI noticed that in the last ten
    meetings you did not speak up once.}~\eref{e1#152}
\end{quote}}

A record chills dissent, or leaves the dissenter marked (L2).
E2 says people become more careful in the phase where reservations are most useful, because
the meeting may be recorded and kept, and the reservation then stays attached to the person
who raised it~\eref{e2#161, e2#162}.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{Jacob had raised reservations at one point, and somewhere it sticks: Jacob has raised
    reservations again.}~\eref{e2#161}
\end{quote}}
E1's formulation is the later use.
Critical comments, which she treats as a condition of a good meeting, can be turned against
the speaker, and that threatens the safe space~\eref{e1#156}.

Calling on a quiet person can shame, or can misread the silence (L3).
E3 says silence is often not a trait of the person.
Naming the one who has not spoken can shame them for not having found a way in.
An intervention can also arrive while they are about to be persuaded, and then leave them
in the view they were leaving.
She does not rule the move out, and she calls it unlikely~\eref{e3#119, e3#120, e3#122, e3#125, e3#127, e3#131}.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{It can be entirely good that the person is saying nothing. Perhaps she is about to
    be convinced by the others. If the AI then barges in, she stays with her view.}~\eref{e3#125, e3#127}
\end{quote}}
E1, asked about a system that invites a person who has not yet spoken, says she would be
very careful about an AI bringing quiet voices in~\eref{e1#159}.
In her own practice, that invitation can feel intrusive online, and naming introversion can
stigmatize~\eref{e1#88, e1#90}.

People will not yield the floor to a machine, especially a senior person (L4).
E3 expects an AI to fail most sharply at interrupting people who want to speak.
The inhibition is higher with a human, including a beginner~\eref{e3#104, e3#106}.
E1 expects a chat to be talked over, because people would rather talk to each other, and she
expects a video agent that tells a senior person they have spoken too long to be
rejected~\eref{e1#178, e1#183, e1#184}.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{Sorry, you have already been speaking too long. Who are you? [\ldots] Especially if I
    were the manager, I would think: forget it.}~\eref{e1#183, e1#184}
\end{quote}}
E2 doubts that a system will be obeyed where it has no accepted authority~\eref{e2#160}.

The system must not decide, and must not impose a structure nobody asked for (L5).
E2 says the decision should not be taken out of human hands, particularly where the solution
is not yet known and the group has to come to a judgment~\eref{e2#166}.
E3 would have the participants trigger the decision mode, or have the system speak
afterwards.
A light that says a decision still seems open, which the group can choose to follow, is the
most proactive form she will consider, and even that might damage the
conversation~\eref{e3#101, e3#108, e3#109}.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{An AI that barges in on its own and imposes a structure the participants did not
    ask for. I find that very hard to imagine.}~\eref{e3#111}
\end{quote}}

A transcript is not enough context for a sound intervention (L6).
E3 says a sound reading would require the decision brief, video, tone, facial expression,
gesture, the history of this group, and the meetings before and after this one.
A transcript does not supply them~\eref{e3#123, e3#133}.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{A great deal more has to be clear than just the meeting transcript.}~\eref{e3#133}
\end{quote}}
E1's case is a person who is only half present because a child is ill.
The facilitator knows that because she was told.
She would not have told the system, and it will still produce a reading of how the person
is in the meeting~\eref{e1#161, e1#162}.
E2 says that treating the question at a general level is difficult.
A closer answer would require the kind of organization, and whether the work is remote or
hybrid~\eref{e2#172, e2#173}.

The group has to agree beforehand that interruption is allowed (L7).
E1 requires that the system be introduced, and that the whole group agree that it may
interrupt, together with an explicit decision about what is stored~\eref{e1#199, e1#200, e1#204}.
The timekeeping interruption is, for her, acceptable under that agreement~\eref{e1#172, e1#199}.
{\setlength{\topsep}{0.25\baselineskip}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}%
  \begin{quote}\small
    \enquote{It has to be introduced. And it has to have an agreement of the whole group that it
    may do that.}~\eref{e1#199}
\end{quote}}

\subsection{Relation to the challenges in Chapter~2}
\label{sec:interview-relation-to-challenges}

The interviews were not designed as a test of the literature, and they are not reported as
one.
Three contacts are close enough to state.

The failure of goal clarity and structure
(\autoref{chal:clear-goals-and-structure}) is present in all three
accounts~\eref{e1#26, e2#28, e3#86}.
The experts add a location.
The unclear goal is often already true in the invitation and in the briefing, and the
structure that fails is often the decision procedure, not only the agenda of the
hour~\eref{e2#30, e2#52, e3#47}.
Unequal participation and hierarchy
(\autoref{sec:key-challenges-in-meetings}) are present as well~\eref{e1#45, e2#112, e3#77}.
The remedy in these interviews is not equal speaking time.
It is a judgment about whose stake matters, combined with a way of speaking that does not
expose the person~\eref{e1#88, e2#112, e3#36, e3#119}.
The groan zone and the diamond
(\autoref{sec:divergent-convergent}) are not only analytic labels here.
E1 describes the diamond as the shape of her workshops~\eref{e1#72, e1#73}.
E2 names the groan zone and the duty not to close it under time pressure~\eref{e2#113, e2#114}.
The neutrality of the facilitator
(\autoref{sec:facilitation}) matches E3's refusal to decide, and it matches E1's demand
that the group take ownership~\eref{e1#137, e3#90}.
E1's consulting proposals show the boundary under strain.
She puts content in front of the group and still leaves the decision with
them~\eref{e1#131, e1#135}.

Two topics that Chapter~2 treats as central are only partly visible in the interviews.
The experts describe practices that seek missing perspectives and that delay evaluation
while positions are still being collected~\eref{e1#71, e2#46, e3#49}.
They do not describe the hidden-profile pattern in which the better option depends on
information that only one person holds.
E2 does propose that an AI might flag a named bias, including the halo effect~\eref{e2#147}.
That is a proposed function, not a report that the bias occurred in a meeting he facilitated.
Technical disturbance is outside the main argument of Chapter~2 and is still in the
material.
E3 names cross-organization tooling and ordinary audio failure as reasons a remote decision
meeting fails before facilitation starts~\eref{e3#20, e3#22}.

What the interviews add, beyond the challenge list used to frame them, is the part of the
decision that an in-meeting moderator does not own.
That part includes who is allowed to decide, whether the participation is real, whether
the meeting is standing in for missing autonomy, whether the people affected are in the
room, and whether the result is owned and checked
afterwards~\eref{e1#45, e2#26, e2#73, e2#122, e2#133, e3#50}.
It also includes the remote loss of informal repair and of tone, and the ethical choice
about what a record is allowed to keep~\eref{e1#201, e3#19, e3#74}.
Those findings remain part of the result whether or not a later prototype implements them.
