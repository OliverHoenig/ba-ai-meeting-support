The system is strongly derived from the Inner Thoughts framework of Liu et al.
Thought formation \cite{liuProactiveConversationalAgents2025}, the evaluation of thoughts, the creation of
thougts, utterance formation, and turn-taking
prediction were adopted to this use case and, in particular, to the live setting. The Timed stimulus pipline
was added, aging and cooldown and the direct answer graph. the rest are concepts form Liu et al
\cite{liuProactiveConversationalAgents2025}.

In the version by Liu et al., the framework is designed for interactions that can be classified as social
chitchat.
That is not what a facilitator would do.
The idea of modeling an AI system through an intrinsic motivation before it contributes anything to a
group discussion nevertheless remains relevant for this thesis.
In particular, the approach indicates higher accuracy in self-selection
(\autoref{sec:proactive-ai-interactions}).

In the discussion, the authors themselves point out that, by adapting the evaluation criteria, the system
can be transferred to task-oriented contexts and can enable goal-oriented proactive action there
\cite["8.2 Apllying Inner Thougs Beyond Casual
Conversations"]{liuProactiveConversationalAgents2025}.
For further work, they propose adapting the system to real tasks with domain-specific heuristics.
The procedure of the original model was therefore largely adopted; the corresponding prompts were adapted.

The system reacts to incoming conversation events in the form of text, based on the live transcript of
what was said.
On every event that is not the system's own utterance, three pipelines with LLM components run
simultaneously (cf.\ 2.4.3 Parallelization). The requests were sent to OpenAI.

They share four stores: the conversation stream, the meeting brief, the memory, and the
thought reservoir.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Timed Stimulus}.
    Based on programmatic preconditions, it reads the full transcript and adds a specialized
    process thought: either a check for shared-information bias and one-sidedness, or a check of
    speaking shares.
    Its task is to make visible risks that a turn-by-turn reaction can miss.
  \item \textbf{Thought Pipeline}.
    It interprets the current event, updates which memories and earlier thoughts are relevant,
    forms new thoughts, and evaluates how strongly each thought justifies an intervention.
    Its task is to keep a reservoir of facilitation impulses ready before a
    speaking opportunity arises.
  \item \textbf{Speaking Pipeline}.
    The decision to take the floor.
    It classifies who the next turn belongs to, answers a direct question from the meeting brief
    when the utterance is addressed to the facilitator, and otherwise speaks the strongest thought
    already in the reservoir or remains silent.
    Its task is to intervene only when the right to speak and the evaluation of the thought
    justify it.
  \item \textbf{Conversation Stream}.
    An ordered record of the speech contributions that are transcribed live during the meeting:
    the contributions of the participants and those of the system.
\end{itemize}

Events are processed serially, one after another.
If the system itself has contributed, that event is not processed again, because the
system could otherwise react to its own contributions.
Every other event starts all three pipelines.
The two scans of the timed stimulus have additional gates and otherwise contribute nothing.

\subsection{Thought Pipeline}

Interpretation, retrieval, and the division into System~1 and System~2 come from the Inner Thoughts framework
\cite{liuProactiveConversationalAgents2025}.

\textbf{Interpret and embed.}
On a human event, the pipeline waits until an interpretation and both embeddings are stored.
The interpretation is a reading of the utterance in one to two sentences, placed before the most recent
transcript.
This was also adopted from \cite{liuProactiveConversationalAgents2025}.

\textbf{Retrieval.}
The salience of long-term memory, short-term memory, and existing thoughts is recalculated against the
current event.
The event is then appended to short-term memory.
This retrieval was also based on \cite{liuProactiveConversationalAgents2025}.

\textbf{Thought formation.}

System~1 and System~2 run together.
System~1 is a fast reaction to the last five events: acknowledgment, pace, a light
inclusion check, or restraint.
It reads only this transcript.
The inclusion check relates to \usref{us:quiet-voices}, and restraint relates to \usref{us:sparse-intervention}.
Whether the thought is spoken is decided only later, by the evaluation and the speaking threshold.
System~2 is deliberate. Two thoughts are prescribed.
The prompt contains the last fifteen events, the meeting brief, up to five salient
short-term memories, and up to three earlier System~2 thoughts.
Thoughts have fewer than 15 words.
The impulses from \usref{us:unshared-information} and \usref{us:hold-dissent} can arise here.

When the heuristics and prompts were adapted, both the literature and the findings from the
interviews were taken into account. The evaluation heuristic for scoring thoughts was adapted so that it
combines the cognitive-psychological foundation of the Inner Thoughts model (based on Grice, Sacks, Goffman, and
Berlyne) with the organizational-psychology principles for compensating shared-information bias (Stasser \&
Titus; Brodbeck et al.) and the HCI guidelines for inclusive facilitation (Houtti et al.; Davison).

In the original Inner Thoughts model of Liu et al., thoughts are evaluated by personal relevance, individual
interests, or the uniqueness of the contribution, criteria that were derived from their think-aloud study.
For informal conversation, this supports authenticity.
For this prototype, the same evaluation would be the wrong requirement:
\usref{us:neutrality} demands substantive neutrality,
and \usref{us:unshared-information} and \usref{us:hold-dissent} demand process thoughts about unshared
information and about open dissent.
An evaluation by personal interests would introduce preferences and thereby repeat the
shared-information bias that the facilitator is supposed to compensate
(\autoref{sec:hidden-profiles}; \cite{schulz-hardtGroupDecisionMaking2006}).
Process thoughts would likely score too low under this heuristic.
The prompts therefore follow the suggestion by Liu et al.\ to adapt evaluation criteria to task-oriented
contexts.
They retain their formation of thoughts and direct it toward the neutrality of the facilitator
(\autoref{sec:facilitation})
and toward the interview practice of opening first, bringing in a missing voice or a missing fact,
and leaving the decision with the group
(\autoref{sec:interview-facilitation-practice}).
Each new thought receives a facilitation motivation from 1 to 5: how strongly a substantively neutral
facilitator without decision-making authority would want to utter it now
(\usref{us:neutrality}, \usref{us:sparse-intervention}; \autoref{sec:facilitation}).
The evaluator sees the last fifteen events and up to ten salient long-term memories.
The prompt retains the structure of Liu et al.\ and replaces personal relevance with this
facilitation motivation.
The wording is in the appendix.
The score is then multiplied by \(1.01\) raised to the number of turns since the last facilitator utterance
and capped at 1 to 5.
This value is the base score \(s_0\), stored together with the time of evaluation.
Hypothesis: longer silence should raise the impulse slightly, without replacing the substantive evaluation.
The base \(1.01\) is a set parameter.
A failed evaluation stores \(s_0 = 0\) and excludes the thought from selection.

\subsection{Speaking pipeline}

\textbf{Turn-taking prediction.}
A classifier reads the last five events and assigns the next right to speak.
The basic structure comes from Liu et al.; the prompt is recast for the facilitator role.
Three classes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{facilitator}: the most recent turn is addressed to the facilitator or asks about process,
    structure, or how to proceed;
  \item \textbf{group}: a named person is addressed, the most recent turn is a domain question, or only
    the participants can carry the subject further;
  \item \textbf{anyone}: the floor is open.
\end{itemize}
The classes serve \usref{us:sparse-intervention} and \usref{us:neutrality}: a domain question stays with the
group, and a process question can lie with the facilitator.
Hypothesis: these three classes separate taking over a content turn from a permissible
process invitation.
They are a set class scheme, not an estimated turn-taking model of these meetings.
The interviews support the direction: the decision stays with the group, and a structure that was not
requested should not be imposed
(\autoref{sec:interview-facilitation-practice}, \autoref{sec:interview-ai-cofacilitator}).

\textbf{Direct answer graph.}
Before a reservoir thought is selected, the system checks whether information is being requested.
Requests for information are answered immediately.
Everything else goes into the decision to speak or to remain silent.
In this way, fixed information stays separate from open facilitation
(\usref{us:goal-clarity}, \usref{us:neutrality}).

The check begins with a text rule and no language model.
It looks for the name or the role of the facilitator, or for a question form.
If both are missing, it ends.
If it finds either, the answer graph starts when the utterance names the facilitator, or when
the predicted right to speak already lies with the facilitator and the utterance is a question.
A question only among the participants does not start the graph.

The graph is a LangGraph state graph built once.
The state contains the utterance, the meeting brief, the name of the facilitator, and the language.
The first step classifies only the intent and forwards it to a call specialized for that intent
(routing, \autoref{sec:ai-agent-building-blocks}).
Five intents:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{meeting facts} (\usref{us:goal-clarity}): agenda, times, goal, current item, and
    attendees, exclusively from the brief.
    Hypothesis: an answer drawn only from the brief prevents the system from inventing a goal that the group
    has not set.
  \item \textbf{decision help} (\usref{us:neutrality}, \usref{us:hold-dissent}): one of majority decision,
    consent, consensus, or a named person who decides after the group has been heard.
    This corresponds to the interview practice of offering a procedure and not taking over the decision
    (\autoref{sec:interview-facilitation-practice}).
  \item \textbf{capabilities}: what the facilitator can do, without reading out the ongoing meeting.
    This makes the role boundary visible and is not assigned to any user story.
  \item \textbf{out of scope} (\usref{us:neutrality}): a fixed boundary answer when a substantive answer
    outside the mandate is requested.
  \item \textbf{facilitate}: none of these requests.
    The graph produces no answer; the speaking pipeline decides on a reservoir thought.
\end{itemize}

\phantomsection\label{sec:dynamic-im}
\textbf{Aging and cooldown.}
In the original, the score remains unchanged after it is produced.
In early development runs, the system spoke thoughts that the further discussion had already overtaken
(\autoref{sec:evaluation-driven-development}).
Re-evaluating every open thought after every event would restore currency
and would make the latency unusable for a live meeting.
The prototype keeps \(s_0\) and reweights it deterministically afterward.
Two hypotheses carry this:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A facilitation thought describes the current process need less well the further the conversation
    has moved since the evaluation, because later information was missing at the time of evaluation.
    Priority should therefore fall with age.
  \item Repeating an intervention that has just been spoken would crowd the discussion.
    \usref{us:sparse-intervention} justifies the cooldown.
    Hypothesis: the motivation for the same process point is very low immediately afterward and can
    return if the group does not take it up.
\end{itemize}
Before the speaking decision, the weighted score is recalculated from \(s_0\) for every evaluated thought.
The time elapsed since the evaluation lets \(s_0\) fall linearly toward zero.
The decay horizon is the straight line that places \(s_{\text{hold}}\) exactly on the
threshold \(\tau\) for an open floor after \(t_{\text{hold}}\) seconds.
The values \(s_{\text{hold}} = 5\), \(\tau = 4.4\), and \(t_{\text{hold}} = 150\) seconds are
calibration parameters.
A thought that has already been spoken is additionally multiplied by a smoothstep of the time since it was
spoken, divided by the cooldown.
The default cooldown is 60 seconds: immediately after articulation the factor is 0, and at the end it is 1.
A thought that was never spoken keeps the factor 1.
The weighted score lies between 0 and 5.
The recalculation also runs when the turn ends through a direct answer.

\textbf{Speak or stay silent.}
The thought with the highest weighted score is spoken if it exceeds the threshold of the
predicted turn class.
The thresholds are adjustable and operationalize \usref{us:sparse-intervention}: the higher the threshold,
the more serious the process benefit must be.
Liu et al.\ consider excessive speaking manageable in their chitchat setting.
Hypothesis: a facilitator must intervene less often than a conversation participant, so the risk of
overcompensation is higher here.
The thresholds control this risk.
They are not evidence that it has already been measured.
The interviews support the caution: do not decide, do not impose unrequested structure, and do not shame a
quiet person
(\autoref{sec:interview-ai-cofacilitator}).

\textbf{Utterance formation.}
The selected thought is verbalized as one facilitation move.
The conditions are the most recent utterance, the more recent transcript (up to 25 events), the stimuli and the
evaluation rationale, the meeting brief, and salient long-term memory.
The move should state the process point and should not set a preference of its own
(\usref{us:neutrality}).

\subsection{Timed Stimulus pipeline}

In the original, Liu et al.\ derive participation only from inner thoughts and treat external cues as a
supplement, not as a replacement for intrinsic motivation
\cite[8.1]{liuProactiveConversationalAgents2025}.
The two scans are this supplement.
They produce process thoughts that a turn-by-turn reaction can miss.
Both then pass through the same evaluation as the thought pipeline.
Hypothesis: because the trigger condition is hard-coded, such thoughts are especially relevant at the moment
they are triggered and are raised by \(0.8\) after the evaluation.
The boost is a set parameter, not an estimated weight.

\textbf{Bias scan.}
The scan serves \usref{us:unshared-information} and \usref{us:hold-dissent}.
Groups discuss shared information more strongly than unshared information, and premature consensus closes the
search too early
(\autoref{sec:hidden-profiles}; \cite{schulz-hardtGroupDecisionMaking2006}).
The interviews describe the corresponding practice: bringing in a missing voice or a missing fact
and not closing dissent prematurely
(\autoref{sec:interview-facilitation-practice}).
E2 would have a system announce a named bias
(\autoref{sec:interview-ai-cofacilitator}).
The gate opens every \(N\) human events.
The default is \(N = 15\); \(N \le 0\) switches the scan off.
\(N\) is a set interval.
The model reads the complete transcript and the meeting brief and forms a thought about the most important
process risk it sees: shared-information bias, overlooked criteria, anchoring, dominant framing,
missing perspectives, or premature consensus.
If the discussion already appears balanced, an observation thought is formed.
The stimuli are the conversation turns.

\phantomsection\label{sec:participation-scan}
\textbf{Participation scan.}
The scan operationalizes \usref{us:quiet-voices} and supports \usref{us:unshared-information}.
Unshared information can be brought in only by the person who has it, and speaking shares are unequally
distributed by status
(\autoref{sec:hidden-profiles};
\cite{stasserExpertRolesInformation1995,bakerEnhancingGroupDecision2010}).
The interviews invite quiet people and warn that doing so online can stigmatize
(\autoref{sec:interview-facilitation-practice}, \autoref{sec:interview-ai-cofacilitator}).
The scan therefore produces a participation thought and not a direct address.
Whether it is spoken is decided by the evaluation and the threshold.
The interviews substantiate the risk of shaming.
They do not substantiate that this scan avoids it.

The scan runs at most every \(N\) human words, 250 by default.
It is checked once, when the word count crosses the next multiple of \(N\).
\(N \le 0\) switches it off.
Only words from human utterances are counted.
An equal share is this word count divided by the number of participants.
An unequal distribution is present when one person holds at least 1.6 times or at most 0.4 times
this share.
An invitation (\usref{us:quiet-voices}) requires only a low share and continued silence.
Curtailment is not assigned to any user story.
Hypothesis: a high share becomes a process problem only when, at the same time, another person lies below
half of an equal share.
The factors 1.6 and 0.4, the half, and \(N = 250\) are set parameters.

\subsection{Technology Stack: Facilitation Agent Runtime and Meeting Client}

LangChain and LangGraph were selected as the central frameworks for developing the AI facilitator.
LangChain provides a modular toolbox for the efficient development of LLM-based
applications, while LangGraph makes it possible to define and control agent-based workflows.

A purpose-built web application was used to conduct the user study. It functions as a simple
video-conferencing solution. It was implemented with Svelte, SvelteKit, and Tailwind CSS and hosted on a
Hetzner server. mediasoup was used as the WebRTC server and as the SFU.

For now, the AI facilitator works with text only. Transcription of what is said takes place in the meeting
client, and text-to-speech likewise takes place in the meeting client.

\subsection{UX and UI of the Meeting Client}

This is not the focus of this thesis, which is why the meeting client was oriented toward the existing UI of
established meeting tools. The original idea of this purpose-built structure was that the system could thus be
designed more flexibly.

For example, it was indicated both acoustically and visually when the AI agent wants to speak. A visual cue
showed when the agent is forming thoughts, and the background color of the meeting line turned a light blue
when the AI agent was speaking. This was intended to make it unambiguous that the AI is currently speaking.

This purpose-built meeting client also carried the idea of showing users an evaluation window during the study, so that
they could rate the current thought with thumbs up or thumbs down. The functionality is implemented, but it
could no longer be evaluated for reasons of time.
