\section{Discussion of research results and decision to narrow down}
\label{sec:discussion-reduce-scope}

\subsection{Retaining Proactive Facilitation Despite Empirical Concerns}

This thesis is based on a specific premise: an AI system that proactively steps in to moderate a
goal-oriented remote meeting while the meeting is in progress.

Since facilitators also intervene proactively in meetings and facilitation is part of their role, RQ2 does
not ask whether facilitation would be useful in principle. It asks which of the challenges
described in RQ1 such a system could actually address during an ongoing meeting, and what form the
intervention must take.

One could argue that this assumption was too narrow. The literature has already identified a tendency for
meeting facilitators to lean toward systems that intervene less proactively. (See 2.3.2)

The expert interviews also reveal some presumed limitations of proactive, interventionist AI meeting systems,
with experts declaring precisely those proactive approaches, which this study aims to investigate in depth, to
be unpromising.

At the same time, the literature and interviews indicate that proactive intervention is necessary in some
decision-focused meetings with a convergent nature and is common practice among human facilitators. However,
the research approaches examined by the author tend to take a more cautious stance when it comes to AI
systems, which has sometimes resulted, for example, in the AI’s contributions being ignored. (see 2.32)

Furthermore, the limitations implied by the expert interviews, althougt they seem plausible, hypothetical.

The question remains as to how participants in goal-oriented remote meetings would actually perceive a
proactive intervention by an
AI system.
However, in his literature review, the author did not find any systems that had actually attempted to
intervene proactively in a live meeting situation, which is why research in this area, with concrete
implementation proposals, is limited.

Therefore, the author decided to stay with the original premise and attempt to develop an initial AI
prototype that could actually be evaluated in a live meeting situation.
This was not because the concerns or limitations raised were deemed implausible, but rather based on the
assumption that an initial attempt to design such a proactive system would reveal further insights,
challenges, and opportunities that would otherwise have received little attention.
It is hoped that patterns can be derived from these learnings to further develop such proactive moderation systems.

\subsection{Focusing the Design Scope on Hidden Profile Szenarios}

The previous chapters have opened up a broad range of issues. Goal-oriented remote meetings often fail due to
a lack of pace, focus, and decision-making, as well as uneven participation and a skewed exchange of
information (\autoref{sec:facilitation-and-group-decisions}, \autoref{sec:hidden-profiles}).

The expert interviews identify several facilitating behaviors that are applicable depending on the meeting
situation. Developing a system capable of responding to all of these behaviors would be too complex and would
not answer RQ2. Any observed effect could not be attributed to any single design decision.

Some of the experts’ suggestions regarding the potential of AI-supported meeting systems fall outside the
scope. Preparation and documentation (F8, F7) take place before and after the meeting.
Summarization, clustering, and time reminders (F1, F2) are already being addressed by existing tools
(\autoref{sec:existing-meeting-ai-tools}). If a role is missing, facilitators ask the group what that role
would say. Simulating this as in (F6) is a different approach that does not fall under proactive moderation.
A decision-making process (F3) can be initiated by the group as soon as options become apparent.  It does not
require a system that intervenes in the ongoing conversation without being asked.

Therefore, the bias indicator (F5) was selected as a promising approach, as it is most closely aligned with
the research question. At the same time, despite the presumed limitations, it remains justified to pursue
this more proactive approach, as this is where the benefits of a proactive system are greatest. Since the
group may not experience the bias themselves, those who would need to actively provide help might not even be
prompted to do so in the first place, and overly passive approaches could potentially be ignored.

The bias associated with a core task of facilitators is the shared-information bias
(\autoref{sec:hidden-profiles}). In this practice, integrating participants means contributing the opinion or
fact that would change the outcome
(\autoref{sec:interview-facilitation-practice}). This is particularly essential for decision-making.
In the hidden profile, the relevant information is already available, but distributed across the group. Based
on their partial knowledge, each person forms a preference that is inferior to the one the group would form
based on the shared situation.

The Inner-Thoughts Framework provides a technical foundation that can be adapted to this use case.

Consequently, this focus was deemed both promising in the context of the insights gained so far and
technically feasible.
