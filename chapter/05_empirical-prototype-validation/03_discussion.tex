\section{Discussion}

\subsection{The meeting scores held while the agent was disruptive (RQ3)}
\label{sec:discussion-rq3}

\paragraph{Answer to RQ3.}
Knowledge workers perceived the meeting among themselves as intact on all five
Davison dimensions.
They perceived the proactive contributions as a disruption.
They kept the floor, so the disruption stayed with the agent and the meeting
scores held.

\paragraph{Why the meeting scores held.}
The Davison items ask about the participants' meeting with one another: whether
they could follow each other, put ideas forward, discuss openly, answer
questions, and spend the time on the decision
(\autoref{fig:likert-communication}, \autoref{fig:likert-discussion},
  \autoref{fig:likert-status}, \autoref{fig:likert-teamwork},
\autoref{fig:efficiency-share}).
The groups kept that exchange.
Group~2 suggested ignoring the agent after its second contribution.
Group~1 continued across 8 of 19 contributions.
Group~3 waited out the delay and went on with the candidates.
A contribution that is talked across stays outside the exchange those items
record.
That is why the scores hold, including in the group that heard the agent nearly
once a minute and still placed its estimates of serious discussion in the upper
part of the 60\% to 85\% range
(\autoref{tab:intervention-frequency}).
The facilitators had stated the limit this rests on: people will not yield the
floor to a machine
(L4, \autoref{sec:interview-ai-cofacilitator}).
Houtti et al.\ report the companion pattern, a meeting rated positively while
the agent's critical feedback was ignored
\cite{houttiObserveAskIntervene2025}.
The strain shows where an item can count the agent as a member.
Expressing oneself was the soft point on communication: five were neutral and
two agreed that this was difficult.
Five of the twelve felt inhibited by the behaviour of another meeting member,
and the free-text accounts attach that inhibition to the agent.
Working as a team is the softest teamwork item, still agreed by nine of the
twelve, with the low median in Group~3.
Those items left the dimensions favorable.

\paragraph{Difficulties the sessions made visible.}
Three difficulties account for the disruption
(\autoref{fig:likert-agent-2}, \autoref{fig:likert-agent},
\autoref{sec:participant-perception}).
The contributions were too frequent, late, and poorly fitted to the moment.
A turn that arrived after the group had moved on interrupted the exchange.
The agent also mixed up persons and, in one account, attached a negative trait
to the wrong candidate.
Participants kept their own turn while the agent was speaking, and at times
became harder for the other members to understand.
After a few such turns the groups stopped taking the agent up.
Trust seems to have fallen quickly enough that later contributions went
unheard, including contributions that may still have been apt.
A further difficulty sits in the judgment of appropriateness.
A facilitation move can serve the process and still feel wrong to the person
it interrupts.
One participant understood that the interruption had made room for two quieter
members, and still found it unpleasant.
Inside a shared-information bias, the reason for an intervention can be
invisible to the people in the room
(\autoref{sec:hidden-profiles}).
A single contribution is therefore hard to score as simply successful or
simply disruptive.

\paragraph{What held up, and what did not.}
The meeting among the participants held up.
Communication, discussion quality, status effects, teamwork, and efficiency
were perceived as favorable, and most of the time was experienced as serious
discussion.
On the agent, the wording held up: eight participants found it such that
nobody was exposed.
In isolated moments the contributions did the work they were written for.
Participants described a quieter member being brought in, a more even share of
speaking time, and one moment in which the agent noticed that the group held
different information.
Six of the twelve did not accept that the agent spoke without being asked.
The other six were neutral or agreed.
What failed was the conduct of the intervention.
Nearly all participants found the agent disruptive, or judged that it spoke
too often.
Frequency, delay, and poor fit outweighed the facilitative moments.
One participant could recall no useful turn: the agent either repeated what
was already under discussion or returned to points already settled.
A human facilitator who took the floor this often, and this late, would be
liable to the same judgment.

\paragraph{How the participants perceived the agent.}
The participants could see the aim and separated it from the execution.
One account calls the goal laudable, letting everyone speak and bringing in
the information, and places the failure in the prototype
(G2-P4, \autoref{sec:participant-perception}).
Content that would have been useful was experienced as disruptive once it
arrived late.
The agent was also perceived as a further party in the room, and in one
account as following an agenda of its own.
The groups differed in how quickly they stopped listening.
Group~3 attended to the agent more closely at the start.
Group~1 spoke in parallel, and early on considered ignoring it because the
time was short.
Only one participant noted stretches of silence in which an intervention would
have been useful.
The dominant memory is of turns at the wrong moment.

\paragraph{How far these results reach.}
The scores describe the meeting the twelve participants felt, recorded
immediately after a first prototype.
Only Group~2 selected the candidate the full information supports, and that
group recognized the hidden profile on its own
\cite{bakerEnhancingGroupDecision2010}.
The favorable dimensions report that felt discussion.
They are silent on decision quality.
The sample is twelve people in three groups, aged 23 to 35, all of whom
already took part in virtual meetings at work.
Whether that age made the technology easier to tolerate is not tested here.
The high rate of intervention limits what a single contribution can show.
Had the agent spoken less often, a mistimed turn would have been less likely,
and a useful turn easier to hear.
The sessions therefore cannot separate a weak contribution from one that went
unheard because trust had already fallen.
The concerns in the literature, and the limit the facilitators had stated,
were observed in the live meetings.
The sessions describe this prototype.
They leave open whether proactive facilitation is worthwhile under a sparser
and better timed pattern of intervention.

\subsection{Findings for the design of comparable systems}

The observations of the prototype and the user studies yield four findings for
work that develops comparable proactive facilitation systems.

\paragraph{Trace analysis requires its own evaluation effort.}
During prototyping, trace analysis in Langfuse was suited to building an
understanding of the system's behaviour
(\autoref{fig:langfuse}).
For the evaluation of real sessions it was no longer practical in the form
used here.
Each conversation event produces several model calls: the prediction of the
right to speak, further calls in the number of thoughts generated, embedding
calls for retrieval, and the formulation of the selected thought as a speakable
utterance
(\autoref{sec:adapted-inner-thoughts}).
In short sessions this sequence stayed manageable.
In a live meeting, where several participants interact at unforeseeable moments
and in a context that is not known in advance, the volume of traces could no
longer be analysed.
Trace analysis can therefore yield a closer view of the system, provided that
the effort of making the traces analysable is planned from the start.

\paragraph{The intervention rate can mask the effect of the system.}
For a proactive system that intervenes facilitatively in an ongoing group
discussion, the intervention rate is presumably the decisive factor.
This agrees with considerations already present in the literature.
The live sessions made it plain in the prototype under evaluation.
Mistimed turns and too high a frequency can hide the effect of the prototype
on the group.
In the user studies the high rate of interaction led many participants to
ignore the system.
A few ill-fitting interventions seem to have lowered trust far enough that
later contributions went unheard.
Those contributions may still have been apt in substance.
Positive effects of the system can therefore stay invisible in user studies,
because participants no longer take the system up once the intervention rate
is too high.

\paragraph{Laboratory conditions cover the live meeting only in part.}
The procedure described in \autoref{sec:evaluation-driven-development} left
individual shortcomings of the system invisible before the user studies.
The procedure remains suited to detecting departures from a constructed oracle
already during implementation.
The user studies suggest that not every relevant situation was included in
these tests, and that assumptions of the laboratory conditions appeared
differently in the live meeting.
Isolated dialogues do not capture turn-taking, timing, or the group's reaction
(\autoref{sec:edd-limitations}).
Work on comparable systems should therefore plan several iterations with user
tests and adapt the system step by step.
Such tests are costly.
In early iterations a short user test can presumably already suffice to
identify the first weaknesses.

\paragraph{Facilitation logic can be separated from spoken interaction only in part.}
Live meetings place higher demands on response time and on the possibilities
of interaction than chat does, and they raise the complexity of the system.
The Inner Thoughts framework was designed for chatbots, and chat interaction
is asynchronous
(\autoref{sec:proactive-ai-interactions}).
In a meeting, participants expect a reaction to the current context.
That includes situations in which a participant interrupts an ongoing turn of
the AI facilitator.
This requirement concerns the design of spoken AI agents in general.
The assumption that domain-specific decisions about facilitation can be tested
in isolation was confirmed only in part by the user studies.
An architecture that already carries the general interaction functions of a
spoken agent offers a firmer basis on which a facilitation system can be built.
